An inquiry-led practice across systems, governance, and repair

The systems that shape shared life were designed. They can be made visible, tested, remade, and repaired.

Explore the inquiry →Recent work

My practice follows how hidden systems become visible, how alternatives are built before institutions are ready, and how experiments can become governance infrastructure. It moves across art, design, civic technology, public institutions, and land—not to present finished answers, but to make questions visible, test what might be possible, and seed forms of shared life that can endure. Increasingly, the work is turning toward repair, stewardship, and what people and places ask us to sustain over time.

Four threads · An evolving inquiry · Questions tested through practice.

Who gets to set
the rules for
shared space?

“At the very least, we need to strive to understand the art of deep listening, practice patience, and explore unknown spaces.”

— Eunji Kang, Design Decode ↗

Since 2006, I have followed this question through art, design, civic technology, and governance infrastructure. This website is not a record of finished answers. It is a place to gather what the work has taught me, make the questions visible, and plant small seeds for what might be tested, cared for, or carried forward by others. The inquiry is still forming—now turning toward land, repair, stewardship, and the slower work of remaining accountable to people and places over time.

— Eunji Kang ↗ · Seoul & London · 2026

For the first years of my career, “I would prefer not to” was quietly becoming my position. I entered design through the work the industry most readily offered: publishing, advertising, branding, and communication in support of sales. I learned how to make things clearer, more desirable, and easier to consume, but became increasingly uneasy with design as a supporting function for mass production and consumerism. The project ended at delivery, while its social and ecological consequences continued beyond the designer’s responsibility. I began asking whether design could do more than improve what already existed — whether it could question what should be produced, how value was defined, and the rules shaping the work in the first place.

Around 2015, while working independently in London, I encountered the wider Project 00 community and a group of remarkable individuals working at the edges of design, systems, and institutional change. That encounter gave organisational form to questions I had already been carrying. Could design become more than a commercial supporting function — a way of thinking, a framework for collective sense-making, and a means of shaping the conditions in which different futures become possible? Dark Matter Labs became the most important setting in which I could test those questions, first through the London practice and later through the opportunity to establish and shape a locally rooted entity in South Korea. Building the organisation — its missions, relationships, language, and economic reality — became part of the design work itself.

That inquiry is still forming. My practice has moved from visual communication into systems research, civic technology, and governance infrastructure, and is now turning toward repair, stewardship, and long-term transition. I am especially interested in micro-cities, technologies designed for civic value rather than efficiency alone, and small conditions at the edge of dominant systems that might seed deeper change. I no longer think design’s value lies mainly in producing answers. Its deeper role may be to enter early enough that value, participation, and the rules themselves can still be questioned — and to stay long enough to help fragile possibilities endure.
